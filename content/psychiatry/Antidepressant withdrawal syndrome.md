# Definition

- "a set of symptoms that can occur after an abrupt cessation (or marked reduction in dose) of an antidepressant medication that was taken continuously for at least one month.” 
- There are no pathognomonic symptoms ("vague and variable")
- Symptoms based solely on pharmacology, not related to reinforcing effects like drug craving in opioid or alcohol withdrawal. 
- The longer you use them, the more common and long lasting the withdrawal syndrome can be. 

# Onset and Duration

- **Symptoms typically begin 2–4 days** after the last dose of the antidepressant, per the DSM-5-TR
- Generally **peak at 1–2 weeks** following cessation (except for fluoxetine, where the long half-life delays and attenuates the peak). 
- Duration: symptoms are usually self-limited, **typically lasting about a week**, but *can persist for weeks to months in some individuals*, particularly after abrupt discontinuation of short half-life agents.

# Symptoms

- The FINISH mnemonic captures the typical symptom cluster: Flu-like symptoms, Insomnia, Nausea, Imbalance, Sensory disturbances, Hyperarousal.
- A survey of 344 patients found that nearly all (97.7%) reported worsening of at least one of four "core" symptoms: **dizziness, brain zaps, irritability/agitation, or anxiety/nervousness** — suggesting these four symptoms may be more specific to SRI withdrawal than the broader FINISH categories.

# DDx: relapse of anxiety or depression?

- A 2025 JAMA psychiatry meta-analysis showed that discontinuation was NOT associated with worsening depressive symptoms in the first 2 weeks, and that early mood worsening after stopping is more likely withdrawal-related anxiety/hyperarousal, while later-onset depressive symptoms (beyond 2 weeks) more likely represent true relapse.

# Deep Dive

## Brain Zaps

- "Brain zaps" (also called "electric shock sensations," "head shocks," or "brain shivers") are among the most distinctive and distressing symptoms of SSRI/SNRI discontinuation, yet their pathophysiology remains **incompletely understood**. The DSM-5-TR lists "electric shock-like sensations" as a characteristic SSRI discontinuation symptom. Several mechanistic hypotheses have been proposed:
    - **Serotonergic rebound hyperexcitability.** The most robust preclinical evidence comes from a 2024 study by Collins et al., which demonstrated that discontinuation of paroxetine in mice triggers a rebound activation of 5-HT neurons. This rebound hyperexcitability is analogous to withdrawal states seen with other psychotropic drugs (e.g., GABAergic rebound after benzodiazepine withdrawal) and may generate paroxysmal bursts of neural activity experienced as "zaps.”
    - **Serotonin-vestibular-oculomotor interaction** — Brain zaps are frequently triggered or worsened by **lateral eye movements**, which has led to the hypothesis that they involve serotonergic modulation of the vestibular nuclei and oculomotor circuits. Serotonin receptors (particularly 5-HT1A and 5-HT2) are densely expressed in the vestibular nuclei and cerebellum. The abrupt loss of serotonergic input to these circuits may produce aberrant signaling experienced as both dizziness/vertigo and the "zap" sensation. PET data showing that SSRIs produce abnormal eye movements during NREM sleep (persisting even after discontinuation) support the notion that serotonergic modulation of oculomotor circuits is profoundly altered by chronic SSRI use.
    - **Noradrenergic contribution** — For SNRIs (venlafaxine, duloxetine), the additional loss of norepinephrine reuptake inhibition may compound the phenomenon. Noradrenergic neurons in the locus coeruleus have extensive projections to sensory cortex, and rebound noradrenergic hyperactivity could contribute to the paroxysmal sensory quality of brain zaps.
    - **Cortical hyperexcitability/ephaptic transmission** — Some authors have speculated that the "electric" quality of brain zaps may reflect brief episodes of cortical hyperexcitability or aberrant ephaptic (non-synaptic) transmission, analogous to the paroxysmal discharges seen in epilepsy. However, EEG studies during brain zaps are extremely limited, and this hypothesis remains largely theoretical.
